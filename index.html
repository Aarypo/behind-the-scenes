Chapter 1 of 7

What the gate hides

Behind this chapter: a single steel bin under a residents' notice board, in a marble lobby.

A gated complex shows the street a clean face: a painted gate, a garden, a security desk. Bins, drains and garbage rooms are kept in basements and back corners, and so are the people who sort and carry the waste. This project asks what the gate keeps out of sight.

Bengaluru now requires every household to separate its waste four ways. We set out to see whether three gated communities in Akshaya Nagar, all within walking distance of each other, have changed their bins, rooms and drains to match.

Introduction by Rizwan

Behind the Gates is a digital deep map of how waste and stormwater are handled inside three gated residential complexes. Each stop on the map holds geotagged photographs, a standard observation record, a smell rating and short notes from interviews. A comparison then sets each complex against the four-stream rule.

Our question is whether a complex that looks well kept actually does what Bengaluru's waste rules require, and what a mapped, photographed, mixed-methods record can show that a walk past the front gate cannot.

Chapter 3 of 7

Why now

Behind this chapter: a padlocked sheet-metal waste room at Block C.

  • The rules have just changed. The Solid Waste Management Rules 2026 have applied since April 2026. They require waste to be separated at source into four streams: wet, dry, sanitary and special care. Bengaluru Solid Waste Management Ltd (BSWML) has told every waste generator in the city to comply.
  • Large complexes carry more of the duty. Big apartment complexes count as bulk waste generators, and the new rules add Extended Bulk Waste Generator Responsibility for them.
  • There is little evidence from the ground. Almost no one has recorded whether gated communities have changed their bins and rooms to match the rules.
  • "Segregation" means two things here. Gated communities separate people as well as waste. We look at where the two overlap: who gets the clean lobby and who works in the basement.
  • It is feasible. The three sites are within about 1.5 km of each other, and the work needs only phones, free GIS tools and online forms.

Chapter 4 of 7

Questions, objectives and outcomes

By Aamina

Four questions

  1. How far does the visible waste infrastructure in each complex (bins, labels, rooms, composting) match the four-stream rule?
  2. Where does stormwater or wastewater collect or smell inside each complex, and how does that relate to ramps, basements and slab joints?
  3. How do residents and housekeeping staff describe segregation in practice, and where do their accounts differ from what we observed?
  4. What can a deep map show about hidden urban waste systems that a written report cannot?

Objectives

  1. Record and geolocate the waste and drainage infrastructure in three complexes.
  2. Score each complex against four-stream segregation using one consistent set of indicators.
  3. Map waterlogging and odour points and link them to how each site is designed.
  4. Put residents' and staff accounts beside the physical evidence.

Outcomes

An interactive deep map, a comparison dashboard, an open CSV dataset with a methods note, and a one-page summary for each residents' welfare association (RWA).

    Chapter 6 of 7

    Consent and care

    Behind this chapter: the group outside the Wing B gate, cropped so that only we are shown.

    The course coordinator approves the topic. Because the project involves human participants through surveys and interviews, we will submit it to the university's research ethics committee. We expect it to count as minimal risk.

    • Access. We will get written or recorded permission from each RWA or facility management before photographing inside again or contacting residents.
    • Informed consent. Every participant sees a statement of purpose, is told that taking part is voluntary and that they can withdraw, and stays anonymous. We record no names or flat numbers.
    • People in photographs. Only group members appear identifiably. Security staff and passers-by are cropped or blurred.
    • Reputational harm. The public map will refer to the complexes as A, B and C and will describe conditions without blaming anyone. Names stay in the internal dataset only.
    • Safety. We will not open manholes, enter shafts or handle waste. Everything is observed from shared areas.
    • Data storage. Raw files are kept in a private shared folder. Only anonymised, aggregated data is published.

    Chapter 7 of 7

    Why it matters

    By Leah

    Social impact

    • Gives RWAs evidence tied to a specific drain or bin rather than a general complaint, now that non-compliance brings fines and refused collection.
    • Records the work of housekeeping staff, which usually goes unseen.
    • Treats standing water and smelling drains as public-health problems inside complexes that look well kept.
    • Offers a cheap phone-and-free-software method that other students or residents can repeat.

    Research value

    Most writing on Bengaluru's waste looks at the whole city: landfills, collection contracts, policy. This project works at the scale of individual buildings, in the months right after the rules changed, and that kind of data is rarely collected systematically.

    Because the survey and interviews sit beside the observations, the project can test the difference between what people say about segregation and the infrastructure that actually exists. The map also leaves a baseline that later cohorts can return to.

    Where this sits within Digital Humanities

    Deep mapping

    Following Bodenhamer, Corrigan and Harris, the map is layered and open-ended. It brings together photograph, measurement, smell and voice instead of reducing the place to a single dataset.

    Data as capta

    Johanna Drucker argues that humanities data is taken, not simply given. Every GPS-stamped photograph involves a choice about what to frame, and the stamp is metadata that becomes part of the evidence.

    Data feminism

    D'Ignazio and Klein ask who counts and who is counted. We ask whose labour is missing from official compliance data, and whose neighbourhoods get photographed at all.

    Discard studies

    Following Liboiron and Lepawsky, we treat waste as a system of power rather than as litter. The gate, the basement and the padlocked room are design choices about what gets hidden.

    The city as a medium

    Shannon Mattern reads urban infrastructure as something that can be studied like a text. We read drains, louvres and bins the same way.

    A methodological commons

    Kirschenbaum describes DH as a place where methods from different fields meet. Here urban planning, sociology, GIS and close reading come together in one public digital object.

    Who sorts the waste is part of the story of the waste. We mean to count them too.
